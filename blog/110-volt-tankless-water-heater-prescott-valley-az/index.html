110 Volt Tankless Water Heaters: A Prescott Valley, AZ Homeowner Guide

A 110 volt tankless water heater plugs into a standard household outlet. It can heat water for a single sink or a short shower, but it cannot supply an entire home. The reason is the amount of power a regular outlet can deliver, and the physics of heating water.

This guide explains what a plug-in unit can really do, shows the math in plain numbers and points out when you need a 240 volt unit or a gas model instead.

Why it is small

The power limit of a standard outlet

A typical household outlet is on a 15 or 20 amp circuit at about 120 volts. A 15 amp circuit supports roughly 1,800 watts at its limit, and continuous loads are commonly held to about 80 percent of that, which is about 1,440 watts. Anything above that risks tripping the breaker.

That power budget is small for heating water. A typical whole-house electric tankless heater uses many times more, drawn from heavy 240 volt circuits.

Temperature rise

The heating math in plain numbers

Heating one gallon of water by one degree Fahrenheit takes about 8.34 BTU. One watt equals about 3.41 BTU per hour. With 1,440 watts you get about 4,900 BTU per hour, which can raise a flow of half a gallon per minute by about 20 degrees.

That is enough to take the edge off cool water for hand washing, but not enough to turn winter incoming water into a hot shower. The cold-season water in a high desert town can start far below room temperature, so the rise needed is large.

Best uses

What a plug-in unit is good for

  • A far-away bathroom sink where the main heater is a long run away
  • A workshop or garage sink
  • An outbuilding where there is power but no hot water line
  • A guest bathroom hand sink
  • Anywhere you need a small amount of warm water near the tap

Choose a point-of-use unit for those spots. It will not run a shower and the dishwasher together, and it may struggle to keep up on a cold morning.

Whole-house

If you want the whole house on tankless

A whole-house tankless unit needs enough power or gas to heat the full flow of the fixtures you run. That usually means a gas unit with a larger gas line and a vent, or an electric unit with heavy circuits and sometimes a panel upgrade. We check those first, since the unit price is often smaller than the supporting work.

Our tankless water heater page covers install, repair and descaling, and water heater installation covers a standard tank swap if tankless does not fit.

Before you buy

Safety and code questions to ask first

Any unit that plugs in still needs a dedicated circuit that matches its label. Sharing the circuit with a microwave or a space heater is how breakers trip and cords overheat. Plug-in heaters should have a ground-fault protected outlet when they sit near a sink, and a licensed electrician or plumber can check that.

Permit rules vary, so ask the Town of Prescott Valley building department whether an added water heater of any type needs a permit.

Comparing small heaters

Mini tank or tankless at one sink

Plug-in tankless

  • Heats only while water flows
  • Small and light, easy to mount under or beside a sink
  • Limited to a low flow and a modest temperature rise
  • No stored water to leak

Small point-of-use tank

  • Stores a few gallons for quick hot water
  • Delivers a steady flow for a short time
  • Takes up cabinet space
  • Needs a relief valve and a drain path

For one far-away sink, a small tank often feels better. It holds hot water ready, so the faucet does not need a high heating load while it runs. A tankless plug-in unit suits a place where you want hot water for only a few seconds at a time.

Neither replaces your main heater. If the question is the whole house, the next step is a proper size calculation, which is what our tankless sizing work covers.

Codes and standards

Electrical work follows the National Electrical Code (NEC), published by the NFPA, which sets circuit and breaker rules for appliances. Ask an electrician or a licensed plumber with electrical scope to confirm that a circuit matches the heater label.

Prescott Valley note: cold-season water

Prescott Valley sits at about 5,100 feet. Average lows run about 25 degrees Fahrenheit in December and January and about 28 in February, based on 1991 to 2020 normals. Water pipes and incoming water are colder in those months, so a heater that seems fine in summer can fall short in winter.

Size any tankless purchase for the coldest month, not the warmest. Ask a plumber to measure your incoming water temperature in winter if you want a precise answer.

A final practical point: label the breaker for any plug-in heater and tell everyone in the house which circuit it uses. If the unit trips the breaker, do not replace the breaker with a larger one. A larger breaker on a wire that is too small is a fire risk, and the right fix is a smaller heater or a new circuit.
