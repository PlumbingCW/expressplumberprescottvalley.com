New Construction Plumbing Rough In in Prescott Valley, AZ

Plumbing in a new home goes in over several stages, each tied to a stage of the build. Underground pipes go in before the slab or footings, rough in follows when the framing is up and final fixtures come last. Each stage is usually inspected before work moves on.

This guide explains what happens at each stage and what a homeowner or owner-builder should settle before the first pipe is set.

Below grade

Stage one: underground

Before a slab is poured, drain lines and sleeves for supply lines are laid in the ground. Their positions are fixed by the plan and cannot be changed after concrete is placed. Mistakes here are expensive to fix.

Bring the final fixture list to the plumber before this stage. Tub, shower and toilet locations all set drain positions.

Framing stage

Stage two: rough in

When framing is complete, the plumber runs supply, drain and vent pipes through walls and floors. Fixture boxes and shower valves are set. The water heater location and gas or electric supply are planned.

Rough in is inspected before walls close. Fix issues now, before drywall.

Finish

Stage three: top out and final

After rough in passes, drywall and finishes follow. The plumber returns to set fixtures, connect appliances and test everything. A final inspection confirms the system works.

Utilities

Water and sewer connections

Before you design, confirm what utilities reach your lot. A lot in a developed neighborhood may have water and sewer at the street. A rural lot may need a well and a septic system. Both require approvals before construction.

Ask the Town of Prescott Valley for guidance on what applies to your parcel, and call the county for lots outside town limits.

Before you sign

Questions to ask your plumber and builder

Settle these in writing

  • ✓Who pulls the permit and who requests each inspection
  • ✓What fixtures and models are included in the price
  • ✓Who supplies the water heater and where it will go
  • ✓Whether the home will have a recirculation line or a softener loop
  • ✓How shutoff valves and cleanouts will be labelled and reached

Clear answers up front avoid change orders later. If a plumber is vague about inspections or who is responsible for permits, take that as a warning sign.

Ask to see a copy of the plan with each fixture marked, and check it against your own drawings.

Future needs

Plan for the home you will live in

A new build is the cheapest time to add features you may want later. A stubbed-out gas line for an outdoor kitchen, a spare capped line for a future bathroom or a dedicated line for a water treatment system all cost little when the walls are open.

Also think about winter. Hose bibs, exposed lines and garage plumbing are common freeze points. Insulate them during construction, not after the first cold night. For help in nearby towns, see Dewey-Humboldt.

Codes and standards

New plumbing is inspected under the locally adopted plumbing code, either the UPC from IAPMO or the IPC from ICC. Ask the Town of Prescott Valley building department which applies and how inspections are requested.

Prescott Valley note: platted lots and growth

Prescott Valley Inc. began selling home lots in 1966. The town incorporated in 1978 and grew from 244 residents in 1970 to 46,785 in 2020. Some lots that were platted decades ago are still being built on, and utility access can differ from lot to lot.

If you own an older platted lot, confirm water and sewer availability with the Town before drawing plans. The answer affects your plumbing layout, your water heater location and your budget.

Appliances

Water heater, gas and venting during the build

The water heater should be planned with the rough in, not after. A gas heater needs a gas line, combustion air and a vent that runs up through the roof. An electric heater needs a dedicated circuit. A heat pump unit needs space and a condensate drain.

Our water heater installation page covers what a good install looks like, and our tankless water heater page explains the extra gas and venting work that on-demand units often need.

Inspection day

Testing and what the inspector looks for

Before the inspector arrives, the plumber tests the drain and supply systems. Drain lines are filled with water or tested with air to look for leaks. Supply lines are pressurized and held. The inspector reads the gauge, looks at pipe supports, slopes and vent connections and signs off.

If the system fails, the plumber fixes the problem and the inspector returns. Staying in touch with both avoids delay. Ask what the local inspection process looks like before you start.

What goes wrong

Common new-build plumbing problems

  • Underground drains set at the wrong depth or slope before the slab is poured
  • Fixture locations that changed after the rough in
  • No shutoff valves or cleanouts where a future plumber can reach them
  • Hose bibs and garage lines left exposed to freezing
  • A water heater location chosen late with no room for venting or a drain pan

Each one is easier and cheaper to solve at the plan stage. A walk through with the plumber before concrete is poured is worth an hour of your time.

Keep a folder with the plan, the permit, the inspection sign-offs and photos of every wall before drywall. Future repairs and resale both go smoother with that record.
