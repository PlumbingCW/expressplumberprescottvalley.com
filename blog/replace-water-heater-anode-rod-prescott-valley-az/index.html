How to Replace a Water Heater Anode Rod in Prescott Valley, AZ

An anode rod is a metal rod inside your tank that corrodes so the steel tank does not. When the rod is used up, the tank starts to rust. Checking it every few years is the cheapest way to extend the life of a tank water heater, and replacing it is a job most handy homeowners can do in an hour or two.

This guide covers how to tell when the rod needs replacing, the tools involved and the points where a plumber is the safer choice. It also covers what to do when the rod will not budge.

The basics

What an anode rod actually does

Inside a steel tank, a thin glass lining protects the metal. Tiny gaps in that lining let water reach the steel. The anode rod is made of magnesium or aluminum, and it corrodes first because it is more reactive than steel. As long as the rod has metal left, the tank is protected.

Rods are consumed over time. Hard water and warm water speed that up. A rod that is thin, bare in spots or coated in white scale is near the end.

Signs

How to tell it is time

Signs the rod is worn

  • ✓The tank is more than three or four years old and the rod has never been checked
  • ✓The rod looks pencil-thin or has bare steel showing at the core
  • ✓Hot water has a rotten egg smell, which can point to a reaction at the rod
  • ✓Rust-colored hot water appears but cold water runs clear
  • ✓Popping sounds from scale on the tank floor
Before you start

Tools and prep

  • A 1-1/16 inch socket and a long breaker bar or an impact wrench
  • A new anode rod that matches your tank, or a flexible segmented rod if headroom is tight
  • Teflon tape or pipe thread sealant rated for potable water
  • A garden hose and a bucket
  • Gloves and a helper if the tank is tight against a wall

Turn off the power or the gas first. Close the cold water inlet valve, open a hot faucet to let air in and drain a few gallons from the tank so the water level drops below the rod. The rod is usually on the top of the tank under a cap or fitted into the hot water outlet.

Steps

Replacing the rod

1

Loosen the old rod

Fit the socket and use steady pressure on the breaker bar. Rods are often tight and need a firm pull.

2

Pull it out

You need about six inches or more of headroom above the tank. A segmented rod bends if headroom is short.

3

Wrap and install the new rod

Wrap the threads and thread the new rod in by hand, then tighten with the socket.

4

Refill and check

Open the inlet valve, let air out through a hot faucet and watch for leaks before restoring power or gas.

Stuck rods

When the rod will not come out

A rod that has been in for many years can be seized in the tank. Forcing it can damage the tank threads or break the rod head. If the rod will not turn after steady pressure, stop. At that point the tank is likely near the end of its life, and replacement may make more sense than a repair.

Common mistakes

Mistakes that turn a simple job into a leak

  • Skipping the drain-down, which lets water run down your arm when the rod comes out
  • Forgetting thread tape, which can leave a weeping joint at the top of the tank
  • Using a regular socket that is too short and slipping off the hex head
  • Overtightening a new rod until the tank threads strip
  • Restoring power before the tank is full, which can burn out an electric element

The last point matters most. An electric heating element that fires while it is out of water fails within seconds. Always open a hot faucet and wait for a steady stream before you switch the breaker back on.

Gas units have a similar rule. Make sure the tank is full before you relight the pilot or reset the gas control.

Reading the rod

What the rod looks like at each stage

ConditionWhat you seeWhat to do
HealthyThick, mostly smooth metal with light pittingPut it back and check again in two years
WornThin in places, flaking, core wire showing in spotsReplace it soon
SpentBare steel core, pencil-thin or broken piecesReplace it now, and flush the tank
Coated in white scaleHard buildup covering the rodReplace it and consider a flush or a filter

If pieces of rod are in the bottom of the tank, flush the tank after you finish. A garden hose on the drain valve will carry most of the fragments out.

Keep the old rod and compare it to the new one. The two should be the same length and have the same head.

Codes and standards

According to most tank makers, the anode rod is a maintenance item that your manual describes, and removing or replacing it should follow those instructions. Tanks are built to ANSI Z21.10.1 for gas and UL 174 for electric.

Prescott Valley note: tank age and winter lows

The 2020 census counted 21,276 housing units in Prescott Valley, and the town grew from 8,858 residents in 1990 to 38,822 in 2010. Many tanks installed in that growth window are now old enough that an anode check is worth doing. If you have never checked yours, put it on the list.

Ask your water utility for its annual water quality report, or test your tap, to see how hard your water is, since hardness is the main driver of rod wear.

Alternatives

Powered anodes and other options

If your water has a rotten egg smell, a different rod material or a powered anode may help. A powered anode uses a small electric current and does not wear out like a metal rod. It costs more up front, and it needs power at the heater.

We also see homeowners add an in-line filter to reduce what reaches the tank. If you are weighing a bigger change, water softener and filtration is the next page to read.
