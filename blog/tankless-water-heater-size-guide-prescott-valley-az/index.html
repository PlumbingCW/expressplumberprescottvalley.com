Tankless Water Heater Size Guide for Prescott Valley, AZ Homes

Sizing a tankless water heater comes down to two numbers: how many gallons per minute you need and how many degrees you need to raise the water. Get either wrong and you will run out of hot water at the worst time.

This guide shows how to add up your fixtures, how to find the temperature rise and why you should size for winter.

Gallons per minute

Step one: add up your peak flow

List the fixtures that might run at the same time. A shower and a kitchen sink at once is a common peak. Add their flow rates to get the gallons per minute your heater needs to supply.

Federal rules cap new showerheads at 2.5 gallons per minute, and many run lower. Check your own fixtures, since older ones may flow more.

FixtureTypical flow range (gpm)Notes
Shower1.5 to 2.5Per showerhead
Bathroom sink0.5 to 1.5Hot side only
Kitchen sink1.5 to 2.2Hot side only
Dishwasher1.0 to 2.0During fill
Clothes washer1.5 to 3.0Hot cycle
Degrees

Step two: find the temperature rise

Temperature rise is the target hot water temperature minus the incoming water temperature. If the incoming water is 50 degrees and you want 120, the heater needs to raise it by 70 degrees. If the incoming water is 70, the rise is only 50.

A heater is rated for a flow at a given temperature rise. At a larger rise, the same unit delivers fewer gallons per minute.

Cold season

Why winter decides

Incoming water is coldest in winter, so the rise is largest and the heater delivers the least. A unit sized for summer can fall short in January. We size for the coldest month so you do not run short.

Our crews measure incoming water temperature during a site visit when needed, which gives a more accurate result than a guess.

Sizing checklist

  • ✓List the fixtures that run together at peak
  • ✓Add their flow rates in gallons per minute
  • ✓Find the coldest incoming water temperature
  • ✓Choose a target of about 120 degrees Fahrenheit
  • ✓Match to the unit rating at that rise
  • ✓Confirm the gas line or electrical service can feed the unit
Run the numbers

A worked example

Suppose a household runs one shower at 2.0 gallons per minute and a kitchen sink at 1.5 gallons per minute at the same time. That is 3.5 gallons per minute of peak hot water flow. If winter incoming water is 45 degrees and you want 120 degrees, the rise is 75 degrees.

You would look for a unit that can deliver about 3.5 gallons per minute at a 75 degree rise. Many units deliver less at that rise, so a larger model or a second unit may be needed.

Avoid these

Common sizing mistakes

  • Sizing by the number of bathrooms instead of peak simultaneous flow
  • Using summer incoming water temperature
  • Forgetting that the gas line may be too small
  • Choosing a unit rated at a smaller temperature rise than you need
  • Ignoring a low flow tap that never triggers the unit

A good plumber asks about your habits, such as whether two showers often run at once. If a quote skips that conversation, ask for the calculation. For service in nearby towns, see Chino Valley.

Codes and standards

Flow rates in this guide follow the federal limit of 2.5 gallons per minute for new showerheads, according to the U.S. Department of Energy. Makers publish a flow chart by temperature rise in each unit manual, and that chart is the number to trust.

Prescott Valley note: size for the cold months

Prescott Valley sits at about 5,100 feet. The 1991 to 2020 average lows are about 25 degrees Fahrenheit in December, 26 in January and 28 in February. Cold air chills exposed pipes and the incoming water, so the temperature rise a heater must provide is larger in winter than in summer.

We size tankless units for the coldest stretch, not for a July afternoon. If your plumber quotes a unit that is just large enough for summer, ask what it delivers at your winter incoming temperature.

Fuel choice

Gas or electric at your size

At a larger size, gas has an advantage. A gas unit can heat a high flow with a comparatively small burner footprint. An electric unit sized for the whole house needs heavy wire and a large breaker capacity, and many older panels cannot carry it.

If your home has gas, a gas tankless unit is often the easier route. If not, a smaller electric unit for one bathroom may be practical. Ask the plumber to check the panel before you buy.

Check the quote

Questions to ask before you accept a quote

A good quote answers these

  • ✓What flow rate does the unit deliver at my winter temperature rise?
  • ✓Will my gas line and meter supply the unit, or does the line need to be upsized?
  • ✓Where will the vent run and what material is it?
  • ✓Is an isolation valve kit for flushing included?
  • ✓What does the warranty cover on the heat exchanger and the parts?

If the quote leaves out the gas line, the vent or the flush valves, ask. Those items are where the final bill grows.

When in doubt, size up one step. A unit with spare capacity runs cooler, lasts longer and handles the occasional extra shower without complaint.
